How to Free Up Gmail Storage

Short answer: Gmail storage is shared across Gmail, Google Drive and Google Photos, so a full inbox is often only part of the problem. Start in Gmail: search for the biggest emails first, because a handful of large attachments usually frees more space than a thousand small messages. Then empty Spam and Trash, unsubscribe from the newsletters you never read, and check Drive and Photos for large files you no longer need. Do it on a computer if you can — the search and bulk-delete tools are much easier there than on a phone. And do not delete in a panic: check what you are deleting first, because deleted mail is gone for good after thirty days in Trash.

First: see what is actually using the space

Open Google's storage page in a browser and sign in with your account. It shows a simple breakdown: how much Gmail, Drive and Photos are each using, and how much is free. If Drive is using most of the space, cleaning Gmail alone will not fix the warning. If Photos is the biggest, the fix is in Photos, not in your inbox. Note the numbers before you start, so you can see whether your cleaning actually worked. Check this page once every few months, instead of waiting for the "storage full" warning, which usually arrives when someone is trying to send you something important.

1. Delete the biggest emails first

In Gmail on a computer, click in the search box, then click the filter icon on the right. Set "Has attachment" and set the size to larger than 10 MB, then search. Gmail shows your biggest emails first — usually old emails with photos, videos, PDFs and presentation files attached. Tick the ones you do not need and delete them in batches. One email with a 25 MB video attached frees as much space as thousands of plain text emails, so start at the top of this list and work down. If an attachment matters but the email does not, download the attachment to your computer first, then delete the email.

2. Empty Spam and Trash — and check them first

Deleted emails sit in Trash for thirty days before Google removes them, and they still count towards your storage until then. Open Trash, glance through it for anything you deleted by mistake, then click Empty Trash now. Do the same for Spam: open the Spam folder and click Delete all spam messages now. Spam is usually safe to clear in one go, but Trash deserves a quick look first — that is where an important email goes when you tap delete by accident on your phone. After emptying both, your storage figure should drop straight away; if it does not, refresh the page, because the number sometimes takes a few minutes to update.

3. Stop the flow: unsubscribe and filter newsletters

Cleaning once is not enough if a hundred newsletters arrive every week. In Gmail, search for "unsubscribe" to see the mailing lists you are on, open the ones you never read, and click Unsubscribe at the top of the email — it takes a few days to take effect, but the flow stops. For the ones you want to keep but do not need in your inbox, set up a filter: search for the sender, click the filter icon, choose Create filter, and tick Skip the Inbox, so they are filed away instead of piling up. This step frees almost no space today, but it stops the inbox filling up again next month.

4. Check Google Drive and Google Photos

If Gmail alone did not free enough space, open Google Drive in a browser, sort by storage used, and look at the biggest files — old video files, large backups, and files shared with you that you no longer need are the usual suspects. Delete what you do not need, then empty the Drive trash as well, because Drive trash also counts until it is emptied. Then open Google Photos, and check for backed-up videos, duplicate photos, and screenshots you took once and never looked at again. If your phone is backing up every photo and video automatically, that is often where the storage goes — switch the backup quality or turn off backup for folders like Screenshots and WhatsApp images, which are rarely worth keeping forever.

5. Archive instead of deleting, where it matters

Not everything needs deleting. Archiving removes an email from your inbox without deleting it, so it still counts towards storage — archive does not free space, and that trips people up. So use archive only for mail you want to keep and find later, and delete for mail you truly do not need. If you are not sure about an email, download its attachment, or forward the email to a second account, before you delete it. And before any big clear-out, check that your important mail is backed up somewhere: a second account, or a download of the emails that matter. Deleting is fast and permanent after thirty days, so a five-minute check before a big delete is always worth it.

Common mistakes to avoid

The biggest mistake is deleting thousands of small emails one by one while ignoring the few large attachments that hold most of the space — always start with the biggest files. Another is emptying Trash without looking, and losing an email you deleted by accident. A third is paying for more storage before checking Drive and Photos: extra storage helps, but if the real problem is a phone backing up every video it takes, the new space fills up too.

Quick checklist

Check the storage breakdown first, then delete emails larger than 10 MB with attachments, and empty Spam and Trash. Unsubscribe from newsletters you never read, check Drive and Photos for large files, and empty their trash too. Keep important attachments by downloading them first.

Frequently asked questions

What happens when Gmail storage is full? You cannot send or receive new emails, and Drive and Photos stop backing up too. Nothing new arrives until you free space or buy more storage.

How long do deleted emails stay in Trash? Thirty days. After that Google deletes them permanently, and they cannot be recovered, so check Trash before you empty it.

Should I just pay for more storage? It is a fair option if you genuinely need the space, but check Drive and Photos first. A phone backing up every video will fill paid storage too — cleaning first usually buys you months.
