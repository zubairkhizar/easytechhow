How to Fix Wi-Fi That Keeps Disconnecting

There is a special kind of frustration reserved for a video call that freezes halfway through a sentence, or a download that restarts for the third time. Wi-Fi that drops again and again is rarely a mystery, though it feels like one. In most homes the cause is one of a short list: a tired router, interference from neighbours, a corrupted saved network, or a driver that needs updating. Work through the steps below in order, and test the connection for a few minutes after each one. The goal is to stop as soon as it works, not to change everything at once.

First, work out whether it is one device or all of them

This single question saves hours. If every phone, laptop and tablet in the house drops at the same time, the problem is almost certainly the router or the internet line coming into your home. If only one laptop drops while everything else stays connected, the problem is on that device: its driver, its saved network profile, or its power settings. Ask someone else in the house, or check your phone while the laptop is struggling. That answer tells you which half of this guide matters most.

1. Restart your router properly, and your device too

Unplug the router from power, wait a full 30 seconds, plug it back in, and give it two minutes to settle. A quick flick of the power button does not always clear the router's memory; the 30-second wait matters. Restart your phone or laptop at the same time. This clears temporary glitches on both ends and gives you a genuinely fresh connection. If the drops stop for a day and then return, note that pattern, because it points to the router overheating or struggling under load, which is worth knowing before you buy anything.

2. Move closer and reduce interference

Walls, microwaves, baby monitors, cordless phones and neighbouring networks all interfere, especially on the 2.4 GHz band, which travels further but is far more crowded. Move closer to the router for a test. If the connection becomes stable at close range, distance or interference was the problem, not a broken device. If your router offers a 5 GHz network, try that for less crowding, bearing in mind that 5 GHz does not travel through walls as well. In a small flat, 5 GHz is often the better choice; in a large house, 2.4 GHz may reach further.

3. Forget the network and reconnect

On your device, forget the Wi-Fi network and reconnect with the password. This removes a corrupted saved profile, which is a surprisingly common cause of repeated drops, especially after a router password change or a software update. It is a two-minute job and it costs nothing. If you have to do this every week, something deeper is wrong, but as a one-off fix it works more often than people expect.

4. Update your Wi-Fi driver or system software

On Windows, open Device Manager, expand Network adapters, right-click your Wi-Fi adapter and choose Update driver. On a phone, install the latest system update. Driver bugs are a classic cause of drops that started suddenly after an update. While you are in Device Manager, also check the adapter's Power Management tab and turn off "Allow the computer to turn off this device to save power". Laptops sometimes put the Wi-Fi adapter to sleep to save battery, and a sleeping adapter looks exactly like a dropped connection.

5. Change the Wi-Fi channel

If many neighbours use the same channel, your network has to shout over theirs. Log in to your router settings, usually at 192.168.1.1 or 192.168.0.1, and look for wireless settings. On 2.4 GHz, channels 1, 6 and 11 are the only ones that do not overlap, so pick the least crowded of those. Many routers have an automatic channel setting that is supposed to do this for you, but it does not always work well. A free Wi-Fi analyser app on your phone can show you which channels your neighbours are using, which makes the choice obvious.

6. Check the router itself

Routers age. If yours is more than five years old, runs very hot, or needs restarting every few days, it may simply be struggling. Make sure it has airflow around it and is not buried behind a television or inside a closed cabinet. Try a different ethernet cable between the router and the wall socket if you use one, because a damaged cable can cause drops that look like a Wi-Fi problem. If the router's lights go out or turn red during a drop, that points to the internet line itself, and it is time to call your provider.

Common mistakes to avoid

Do not factory-reset the router as a first step; that wipes your settings and password and rarely fixes a simple interference problem. Do not buy a Wi-Fi extender before you know whether the problem is coverage or interference, because an extender can make interference worse. And do not change five settings at once, because then you will never know which one actually helped.

If nothing works

Reset network settings on the device as a last resort. This removes saved Wi-Fi networks and Bluetooth pairings, so you will need passwords again, but it clears deep configuration problems. If every device still drops, contact your internet provider and tell them the pattern: when it drops, whether the router lights change, and whether a wired connection also drops. That detail gets you past the usual "have you tried restarting it" script much faster.

Frequently asked questions

Why does Wi-Fi drop only in one room? Usually distance, walls, or interference in that room. A 5 GHz signal in particular fades quickly through thick walls, so the 2.4 GHz network may be steadier there.

Should I use 2.4 GHz or 5 GHz? Use 5 GHz for speed at close range and 2.4 GHz for longer range through walls. Many routers let you try both under different network names.

Does a VPN cause Wi-Fi drops? A VPN can cause slow speeds and occasional disconnects, but it rarely causes the Wi-Fi itself to drop. If drops stop when the VPN is off, the VPN is the suspect, not the router.

When should I replace the router? When it needs frequent restarts, runs very hot, or cannot keep up with the number of devices in your home, even after the steps above.
