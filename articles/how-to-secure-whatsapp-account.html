How to Secure Your WhatsApp Account

WhatsApp accounts are a popular target for hijacking, because taking over one account gives an attacker access to your contacts, your groups and your conversations. The attack usually starts with a trick, not with hacking. Someone messages you pretending to be a friend or a support agent, and asks you to share a code that just arrived by text. These steps close that door, and they take about five minutes in total, which is a good trade for the account most people use every day.

How WhatsApp accounts get hijacked

The most common attack is simple social engineering. Someone messages you pretending to be a friend or a support agent, and asks you to share a six-digit code that just arrived by SMS. That code is actually the code to register your WhatsApp number on their phone. Once you share it, your account moves to their device and you are locked out. Another route is a linked device left logged in on a shared computer, or a weak phone lock screen that lets someone read your messages. Understanding these routes matters, because every step below blocks one of them directly, and none of them requires technical skill.

1. Turn on two-step verification now

This is the single most important step. In WhatsApp, go to Settings, then Account, then Two-step verification, and tap Enable. Choose a six-digit PIN you will remember, and add an email address for recovery. From now on, registering your number on any new phone requires this PIN as well as the SMS code, so an attacker who tricks you into sharing the SMS code still cannot take over your account. Do not use a PIN like your birth year or 123456, and do not share it with anyone, even someone claiming to be from WhatsApp support. WhatsApp will never ask you for this PIN, and anyone who does is trying to trick you.

2. Never share a verification code with anyone

Make this a hard rule, with no exceptions. A genuine friend will never need your WhatsApp code, and neither will support. If a message asks for a code "to verify your identity" or "to fix your account", treat it as an attack, even if the sender's photo looks familiar, because hijacked accounts are used to trick their contacts in exactly this way. If you ever share a code by mistake, act immediately: try to re-register WhatsApp on your own phone, which kicks the other device out, and tell your contacts that messages from your account during that time may not have been from you.

3. Check linked devices

In WhatsApp, go to Settings, then Linked devices, and look at the list. You should recognise every entry: your own computer, perhaps a tablet. If you see a browser or device you do not recognise, or a login from a place you have never been, log it out immediately. Also log out of any old computer you no longer use, because a linked device left on a shared or sold computer is an open door. Make this check part of your routine every few months, and always after using WhatsApp on someone else's computer.

4. Lock your phone properly

Your WhatsApp security is only as strong as your phone lock. Use a PIN, pattern, fingerprint or face lock that someone picking up your phone cannot guess. Turn on WhatsApp's own screen lock as well, if your phone supports it, so that even an unlocked phone does not open straight into your chats. And set message previews so they do not show full text on the lock screen, because a preview can reveal a verification code to anyone glancing at your phone.

5. Tighten privacy settings

In Settings, then Privacy, review who can see your last seen, profile photo, about information and status. Setting these to "My Contacts" rather than "Everyone" reduces the information strangers can gather about you, which makes social engineering harder. You can also control who can add you to groups, which stops strangers adding you to spam groups. These settings do not make you invisible to your friends, but they do make you a harder target for someone scanning for victims.

6. Back up safely and stay alert

Turn on encrypted backup if it is offered in your version of WhatsApp, so your chat backup is protected by a password or key, not just stored in plain form. And stay alert to the oldest trick in the book: a message from a "friend" in trouble asking for money or a code. If a request feels urgent and out of character, call that friend on a number you already know, rather than replying in the chat. That single phone call defeats most hijacking attempts, because the attacker cannot answer it.

Common mistakes to avoid

Do not use a weak two-step PIN, do not share codes for any reason, and do not leave WhatsApp Web logged in on a shared computer. Do not assume that because a message comes from a familiar name, it comes from that person; hijacked accounts look identical. And do not delay turning on two-step verification until after something goes wrong, because it only protects you if it is already on.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between the SMS code and the two-step PIN? The SMS code proves you own the phone number at registration time. The two-step PIN is an extra secret you set yourself, required as well, so a stolen SMS code alone is not enough.

What should I do if my account is hijacked? Re-register WhatsApp on your own phone immediately, which removes the other device, then turn on two-step verification and warn your contacts.

Does WhatsApp ever ask for my code or PIN? No. WhatsApp support will never ask you to share a verification code or your two-step PIN.

Should I use encrypted backup? Yes, if it is available to you. It protects your backed-up chats with a password or key, rather than leaving them readable in cloud storage.
