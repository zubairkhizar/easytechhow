How to Protect Your Google Account in 15 Minutes

Your Google account is probably the most valuable account you own, and most people do not realise it until something goes wrong. It guards your email, your photos, your documents, and very often the password resets for everything else. If someone gets into it, they can reset many of your other accounts too. The good news is that fifteen minutes of setup, done once, makes that dramatically harder. This guide walks through those fifteen minutes in order, starting with the step that matters most.

Why this matters more than you think

Think about what a stranger could do with your email alone. They could read private messages, find out where you bank, and request password resets for shopping, social media and work accounts. That chain reaction is why account security is not really about one password; it is about making sure a single stolen password is not enough. The steps below add layers, so that even if one layer fails, the next one stops an intruder.

1. Use a strong, unique password

Your Google password should be long and used nowhere else. That last part is the one people skip, and it is the one that matters. When a smaller website is breached, attackers try the same email and password on Google, banking and shopping sites, because they know people reuse passwords. A password manager can generate a long, random password and remember it for you, so you do not have to. If you do not use a manager, pick a passphrase made of several unrelated words that you can remember, and do not reuse it anywhere else.

2. Turn on 2-Step Verification

Go to myaccount.google.com, then Security, then 2-Step Verification, and turn it on. From then on, signing in from a new device needs both your password and a second proof that it is you. An authenticator app or a passkey is better than SMS codes where possible, because SMS codes can be intercepted if someone tricks your phone company into moving your number. Keep backup codes somewhere safe, like a drawer at home, not in a note on your phone. If you lose your phone, those codes are how you get back in.

3. Add a passkey

Passkeys let you sign in with your fingerprint, face or device PIN instead of typing a password. They resist phishing because a passkey only works on the real Google site; it cannot be tricked into working on a fake login page. You can add a passkey from the same Security page, using your phone or computer. You do not have to give up your password entirely, but a passkey makes everyday sign-in faster as well as safer, which is a rare combination.

4. Review signed-in devices

In the Security section, look at Your devices. You will probably see your current phone and computer, and possibly older devices you forgot about: a previous phone, a work computer, a tablet you sold. Sign out of anything you do not recognise or no longer use. If you see a device or location you genuinely do not recognise, change your password immediately and run the Security Checkup. This review takes two minutes and it is worth doing every few months.

5. Check third-party access

Still in Security, review third-party apps and services with access to your account. Over the years, most people accumulate old quiz apps, shopping tools and services they tried once and forgot. Each one is a quiet back door. Remove anything you no longer use or do not recognise. Keep the apps you actually rely on, and remember that removing access does not delete your data from that app; it just stops it reaching your Google account in future.

6. Set recovery options you control

Add a recovery email and phone number that you actually control and can access. If you ever get locked out, these are how Google will verify it is really you. Use a recovery email that is not the same account, and a phone number you will keep for a while. If you change your number, update it here straight away; an old recovery number is worse than none, because it can lock you out or, worse, hand access to whoever gets that number next.

Run a Security Checkup, then stay alert

Finally, run Google's Security Checkup from the same Security page and follow its recommendations. It will flag weak settings you missed. Repeat it every few months. And be suspicious of any email asking you to sign in urgently, especially one that threatens account closure. Google does not ask you to confirm your password by email. When in doubt, do not click the link; type myaccount.google.com into your browser yourself and check there.

Common mistakes to avoid

Do not share verification codes with anyone, even someone claiming to be from Google support. Do not use the same password for Google and your email recovery account. Do not leave old devices signed in after selling or giving them away. And do not write your password on a sticky note on the monitor, which is still surprisingly common in offices.

Frequently asked questions

Is 2-Step Verification annoying to use every day? Not really. Once your own devices are recognised, you are rarely asked again. It mainly appears when you sign in from somewhere new, which is exactly when you want the extra check.

What if I lose my phone? Use your backup codes, your recovery email, or another signed-in device to get back in. That is why setting recovery options before you need them matters so much.

Are passkeys better than passwords? For most people, yes. They are harder to phish and faster to use, because they rely on your device and biometrics rather than something you have to remember and type.

How often should I check my security settings? Every few months is enough, plus immediately after anything suspicious, like a login alert from a place you have never been.
