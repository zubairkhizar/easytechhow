How to Speed Up Windows 11 in 10 Simple Steps

My neighbour once asked why his two-year-old laptop took five minutes just to open a browser. Nothing was broken. It was simply carrying too much baggage: programs launching at boot, a nearly full drive, and a browser with forty tabs held open "just in case". This guide will not turn an old machine into a new one, but it will remove the everyday drag that makes Windows 11 feel slow.

First, find out what is actually slow

Before you change anything, spend two minutes noticing the problem. Is the whole computer slow, or only one program? Does it crawl right after you switch it on, or does it slow down after an hour of use? A slow start usually points to startup programs. A machine that gets worse through the day often has a memory-hungry browser or an app leaking memory in the background. And a computer that stutters when you open files may be running out of storage. Knowing which one you have saves you from changing settings at random, which is how people end up breaking things that were fine.

Open Task Manager with Ctrl + Shift + Esc and look at the Performance tab. Memory above 85 percent while only browsing points to too many tabs or background apps. A disk constantly at 100 percent points to storage or a failing drive.

1. Turn off unnecessary startup apps

Every program that launches at boot adds seconds to your morning. Open Task Manager, go to Startup apps, and look at the list honestly. Chat apps, game launchers, cloud storage tools and printer utilities often set themselves to start automatically, even if you only use them once a week. Disable the ones you do not need immediately after switching on. You are not uninstalling them; you can still open them whenever you want. Restart the computer afterwards and time the difference. For most people this single step is the biggest win of the lot.

Leave security software and anything made by your computer manufacturer alone unless you are sure what it does. If you are unsure about an entry, search its name online first.

2. Uninstall apps you never use

Go to Settings, then Apps, then Installed apps, and sort by size or date last used. Trial software, old toolbars, and games you finished months ago are all candidates. Removing them frees storage and stops background services you never asked for. Be careful with anything labelled as a driver, a system component, or a program from your laptop maker that handles power or updates. When in doubt, leave it installed and just stop it from starting automatically.

3. Free up storage with Storage Sense

Windows needs breathing room. When a drive is more than about 85 percent full, the system struggles to create temporary files and manage memory, and everything feels sluggish. In Settings, go to System, then Storage, and turn on Storage Sense. It removes temporary files, old Recycle Bin items and files in Downloads that you have not touched in a while. Aim to keep at least 15 to 20 percent of the drive free. If you are still short, move large videos and photo folders to an external drive or cloud storage rather than deleting things you might need.

4. Update Windows and drivers

Outdated graphics and chipset drivers are a quiet cause of lag, especially after a major Windows update. Check Settings, then Windows Update, and install what is offered. Then visit your PC maker's support page and look for driver updates for your exact model. Do not download drivers from random websites that promise to "fix" your computer; those are a common source of unwanted software. A driver update will not always feel dramatic, but it removes a whole class of stuttering and crashes.

5. Reduce visual effects on older hardware

Search for "Adjust the appearance and performance of Windows" and open it. Choosing "Adjust for best performance" turns off animations and transparency, which makes menus feel snappier on older machines. On a modern computer the difference is small, so try it for a day and judge for yourself.

6. Check for malware and heavy background apps

Run a full scan with Windows Security, which is built in and does not need a subscription. Malware and unwanted programs often run quietly in the background and eat processor time. While you are there, look at Task Manager's Processes tab sorted by CPU. If one app is constantly using a large share of the processor while you are doing nothing, that app deserves attention. Sometimes the fix is simply updating it; sometimes it is a program you no longer need.

7. Restart regularly, and know when hardware is the limit

A simple restart clears memory leaks and stuck processes. If you only ever close the lid and put the laptop to sleep, restart at least once a week. Beyond that, be honest about hardware. If your PC still uses a traditional hard drive, switching to an SSD is the single biggest speed upgrade available, and it is often cheaper than people expect. Adding more memory helps if you regularly work with many browser tabs or large documents. Neither change is needed for everyone, but no amount of settings tweaks will make up for a drive that is physically slow.

Common mistakes to avoid

Do not install several "PC cleaner" programs at once; they often do more harm than good. Never delete files from the Windows folder or System32, and do not turn off Windows Update to save time.

If a step does not help

Undo it and move on. Startup changes can be reversed in Task Manager, uninstalled apps can be reinstalled, and visual effects can be switched back in seconds. If the computer is still slow after all of this, run the built-in hardware diagnostics from your manufacturer, or ask a technician to check the drive health. A slow computer with a failing drive is not a settings problem, and catching it early can save your files.

Final checklist

Work through the steps one by one, restart, and test the apps you actually use every day. Repeat the cleanup once a month, keep updates on, and keep some free space on the drive. That routine, more than any single trick, is what keeps Windows 11 feeling fast.

Frequently asked questions

Will turning off startup apps delete them? No. Disabling a startup app only stops it launching at boot. You can still open it from the Start menu whenever you need it.

How much free storage should I keep? Aim for at least 15 to 20 percent of the drive free. Below that, Windows struggles with temporary files and updates.

Is an SSD upgrade worth it on an old laptop? Usually yes, if the rest of the machine still works well. It is the one upgrade that makes boot times and file opening noticeably faster.

Should I use a third-party cleaner? For most people, no. Windows already includes Storage Sense, Disk Cleanup and Windows Security, and they cover the same ground without the risk.
